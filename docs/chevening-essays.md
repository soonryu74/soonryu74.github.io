## Essay 1 — Leadership and Influence (약 470단어)

Leadership in public health rarely looks like a podium. In my experience it looks like a telephone that rings at the wrong hour, and the decision about who will pick it up.

I have worked for [22] years in Korea's public health system, first as a registered nurse and then as a civil servant in [infectious disease control / health administration]. Across that time the country passed through SARS, MERS and COVID-19, and I watched an institution grow from a small office that managed outbreaks by fax into a national disease control agency. I was not the person on the podium. I was one of the people who made sure the information reached the podium in time, and that the people who gathered it were not forgotten afterwards.

The moment I would point to is [year], during [MERS / COVID-19 / a regional outbreak]. My unit was responsible for [contact tracing / situation reporting / local response coordination] across [number] local health centres. The formal reporting chain was slow: a case confirmed in the morning could take most of a day to reach the people who needed to act on it. I did not have the authority to change the chain. What I could change was the people. I built a direct list of the individuals in each centre who actually answered the phone, called them myself, and agreed a simple rule: if you are unsure whether to report, report. Within [weeks], our reporting lag fell from [X] to [Y], and the same list was used again in the next outbreak by colleagues who had never met me.

What I learned is that influence in a bureaucracy is built on being the person who remembers names and returns calls, not on rank. I also learned that systems fail quietly, in the gap between a rule and the person expected to follow it, and that a leader's job is to stand in that gap.

More recently I have led in a different way. In 2025 I began using AI coding tools to build the software my work needed but could not procure: a surveillance newsletter tool that pulls from 78 official sources, a regional health dashboard built on national community health survey data, and a searchable database of parliamentary health audits. I built fourteen working services in a year without a development team. I then started a YouTube channel, now with around [10,000] subscribers, most of them over fifty, to teach other public servants and older Koreans to do the same. Several [colleagues / viewers] have since built their own tools.

I want to study in the UK because I have reached the limit of what I can teach myself. I can build a dashboard; I want to know whether it changes a decision. A Chevening year would let me turn two decades of field practice and one year of self-taught digital work into something I can lead with formally, and bring back to a system that still runs too much of its response on telephone lists.

## Essay 2 — Networking (약 460단어)

The most useful network I have ever built has no organisation chart. It is a list of names, phone numbers and a note about what each person actually does when the official description says something else.

Korean public health is formally hierarchical and informally personal. Local health centres report to provinces, provinces to the national agency, and each layer edits what it passes upward. In [year], while working on [surveillance / response coordination], I found that the fastest way to know what was happening in [region] was not the reporting system but a public health nurse I had met once at a training course. She told me things a week before they appeared in any report. I started keeping a list. Over [number] years it grew to cover [most of the 244 / number] local health centres, and it has been passed to colleagues who inherited my post.

That list taught me three things about networking. First, reciprocity comes before request: I called people when I had something useful for them, long before I needed anything. Second, the people who matter are usually two levels below the people who sign. Third, a network is maintained in the quiet months, not during a crisis.

I have used the same approach outside government. When I began publishing health surveillance newsletters, I wrote to the authors of the sources I cited, at [WHO / ECDC / national agencies / Korean academic institutions], to ask whether I had read them correctly. Several replied, and two became regular correspondents who now send me material before it is published. When I built a database of parliamentary health audits, I contacted [parliamentary staff / journalists / researchers] who had spent years requesting those documents by hand, and the database improved because they told me what was missing.

My YouTube channel is a network of a different kind. The audience is mostly Korean adults in their fifties and sixties who want to use AI tools for work and small businesses. I answer comments personally, and the questions that recur become the next video. Some viewers now teach others. I did not plan this as networking, but it is the same principle: give first, listen for what people actually need, and keep showing up.

In the UK I would network deliberately. I would seek out the Chevening alumni working in health systems in [Southeast Asia / East Asia], where Korea's disease control agency already has cooperation agreements, and the UK Health Security Agency staff who teach at [LSHTM / UCL]. I would bring something to those conversations: a working knowledge of how a country went from fax-based outbreak control to a national agency in twenty years, and the mistakes it made on the way. On return I would connect that network to the people on my list, because the gap between international guidance and a district nurse's telephone is still the gap where outbreaks grow.

## Essay 3 — Studying in the UK (약 480단어)

I have applied to three programmes that together cover the question I cannot answer from inside my current job: when public health data is collected well, does it actually change what happens on the ground?

My first choice is the MSc Public Health at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine. LSHTM sits at the centre of the field I have worked in for two decades, and its faculty wrote or reviewed many of the guidelines I have translated and applied. I want to study epidemiology and health policy in the same programme, because in my experience they are separated in practice: epidemiologists produce numbers, and policy is made by people who did not read them. LSHTM's [module names, e.g. Health Policy, Process and Power; Epidemiology and Control of Communicable Diseases] address that gap directly.

My second choice is the MSc Health Data Science at University College London. Over the past year I have built, without formal training, a regional health dashboard from Korea's community health survey, an automated surveillance newsletter drawing on 78 sources, and a searchable database of parliamentary health audits. They work, but I built them by trial and error. UCL's programme would give me the statistical and methodological foundation to know when a pattern in the data is real and when it is an artefact of how I collected it. UCL's Institute of Health Informatics also works closely with the NHS, whose experience of national health data infrastructure is directly relevant to Korea's own single-payer system.

My third choice is the MSc Global Health Policy at the University of Edinburgh. Korea's disease control agency now exports its experience to [Southeast Asian] partners, and I expect part of my future work to be in that cooperation. Edinburgh's programme is explicitly comparative and policy-focused, and its [Global Health Governance Programme / Usher Institute] would let me study how other middle-income countries built their systems, rather than assume Korea's path is the only one.

The UK is the right place for this study for three reasons. First, the UK Health Security Agency and its predecessors have thirty years of published, candid evaluation of their own outbreak responses, including their failures. Korea's evaluations exist but are rarely public; I want to learn from a system that writes down what went wrong. Second, the UK's health data infrastructure, through the NHS and [OpenSAFELY / UK Biobank], is the most developed example of population-scale health data being used for policy in near real time. Third, UK master's programmes are one year. I am [52] and have a career to return to; a one-year programme is a realistic commitment for my employer and for me.

I have considered studying in Korea or the United States. Korean programmes are strong in epidemiology but weaker in policy analysis, and US programmes are two years. A Chevening year in the UK is the option that fits both the question I want to answer and the life I will return to.

## Essay 4 — Career Plan (약 470단어)

My career plan is to spend the years I have left in public service closing one specific gap: the distance between the data Korea's health system collects and the decisions its local health workers make.

Korea collects health data exceptionally well. The community health survey reaches every district every year; disease surveillance is near real time; the national insurer holds claims for the entire population. Yet when I worked in [local response / surveillance], the district nurse deciding whether to visit a household was working from a paper list and a phone call. The data existed. It did not reach her in a form she could use. I have spent the last year building tools to shrink that distance, and I have reached the limit of what self-teaching can do.

In the two years after returning from the UK, I intend to return to [my agency / the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency / provincial health department] and take responsibility for [a data-to-frontline project: for example, a district-level surveillance dashboard that local health centres can actually use]. I already have working prototypes. What I will bring back is the methodological rigour to evaluate whether they change outcomes, and the policy training to get them adopted rather than admired. I will also propose that Korea publish candid after-action reviews of its outbreak responses, as the UK does. I have seen what happens when lessons stay inside the building.

In five years I want to be in a position, whether in the national agency or in a provincial health authority, where I set standards for how surveillance data is presented to frontline staff, and where I train the people who will do this after me. Korea's disease control agency created a formal epidemic intelligence officer programme after MERS; I want to add a digital and data track to that training. I have already taught several thousand people through my YouTube channel, most of them over fifty, to build simple tools with AI. I know it can be taught to people who do not think of themselves as technical.

In ten years I would like to contribute to Korea's cooperation with health systems in [Southeast Asia / the Western Pacific], where Korea is now an exporter of disease control experience. The honest version of that experience includes the fax machines, the MERS failure and the twenty years in between. I would rather partner countries learn from it than repeat it.

I also write. I have completed a two-volume novel about the people who built Korea's epidemic intelligence service, because the public knows the case numbers and not the people. I mention it because it is the same project as my career: making the work visible so it can be improved.

Chevening would give me a year to stop building alone and learn how this is done well elsewhere. I will bring it back. I have nowhere else I want to go.
